Japanese has no word for "that" or "which" the way English does. Instead, a whole clause in its plain form sits directly in front of a noun and describes it, exactly the way a single adjective would.

<JSentenceBlock furigana="私[わたし]たちが晩[ばん]ご飯[はん]を食[た]べたレストランは有名[ゆうめい]です。" romaji="Watashitachi ga bangohan o tabeta resutoran wa yuumei desu." english="The restaurant we ate dinner at is famous." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="一番[いちばん]仲[なか]がいい友達[ともだち]" romaji="Ichiban naka ga ii tomodachi" english="my closest friend" />

<Hint>私たちが晩ご飯を食べた describes レストラン the same way 静かな or 有名な would, an entire clause working as one big adjective. The subject of that inner clause takes が, never は, exactly the rule you met in が・は.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="これは誰[だれ]もいなかった海[うみ]です。" romaji="Kore wa dare mo inakatta umi desu." english="This is a beach where there was no one." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="来年[らいねん]、私[わたし]たちが行[い]きたいビーチはサンセットビーチです。" romaji="Rainen, watashitachi ga ikitai biichi wa Sansetto Biichi desu." english="The beach we want to go to next year is Sunset Beach." />

<Hint>Tense inside the relative clause is independent of the main sentence's tense, past, present, or a want like 行きたい can all describe the noun regardless of when the main clause is set, since the clause is just modifying the noun, not making its own separate claim about time.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="キムさんです。英語[えいご]が上手[じょうず]な韓国人[かんこくじん]の友達[ともだち]です。" romaji="Kimu-san desu. Eigo ga jouzu na kankokujin no tomodachi desu." english="This is Kim. A Korean friend who's good at English." />

<Hint>Introducing a person this way, name first, then a relative clause naming a key trait, is an extremely common pattern in conversation, packing identity and description into one smooth sentence.</Hint>
