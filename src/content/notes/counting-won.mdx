Korean didn't settle on one way to count. There's a native Korean set for things like age and counting objects one by one, and a separate Sino-Korean set, borrowed from Chinese centuries ago, for anything with a fixed, official feel: money, addresses, phone numbers, dates. This lesson is entirely the second kind. The good news is that it's the more regular of the two, built from ten digits and a small set of place values that combine like Lego.

### The Ten Digits

Once you have 일 through 구 and 십, every number up to 99 is just multiplication and addition read left to right. 이십 (2 x 10) is twenty, 이십삼 (2 x 10 + 3) is twenty-three. No "twenty-one, twenty-two" style irregularity to memorize.

One trap: 만 marks *ten thousand*, not a thousand. Korean prices jump from 천 (thousand) straight to 만 (ten thousand) with nothing in between, which is why Korean money always sounds enormous in direct translation. 오만 원 isn't "five money," it's 50,000 won.

<Hint>Once a price is obviously a price, Koreans drop 원 the same way an English speaker drops "dollars" and just says "that'll be five fifty." A barista might just say 오천 오백 (5,500) and trust you to know it means won, not a bare number floating in space.</Hint>

### Floors and Room Numbers

The same digits, attached to a different counter word, cover floors (층), room numbers (호), and bus or gate numbers (번).

<SentenceBlock formal="이백이 호입니다." formalRom="ibaegi hoimnida." polite="이백이 호예요." politeRom="ibaegi hoyeyo." casual="이백이 호야." casualRom="ibaegi hoya." english="It's number 202." />

<SentenceBlock formal="삼백사십 번입니다." formalRom="sambaeksasip beonimnida." polite="삼백사십 번이에요." politeRom="sambaeksasip beonieyo." casual="삼백사십 번이야." casualRom="sambaeksasip beoniya." english="It's number 340." />

<Hint>Phone numbers break the "read it as one big number" rule. 010-3456-7890 isn't a single quantity, so Koreans recite it digit by digit: 공일공, 삼사오육, 칠팔구공. And in that context 0 is almost always 공, not 영, the same way English speakers say "oh" instead of "zero" reading out a phone number.</Hint>

### What Month Is It?

<SentenceBlock formal="몇 월입니까?" formalRom="myeot worimnikka?" polite="몇 월이에요?" politeRom="myeot worieyo?" casual="몇 월이야?" casualRom="myeot woriya?" english="What month is it?" />

<SentenceBlock formal="십이월입니다." formalRom="sibiworimnida." polite="십이월이에요." politeRom="sibiworieyo." casual="십이월이야." casualRom="sibiworiya." english="It's December." />

Months are just number + 월, with two exceptions that break the pattern purely for ease of pronunciation: June is 유월, not 육월, and October is 시월, not 십월.
