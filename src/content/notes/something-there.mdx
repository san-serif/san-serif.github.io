English uses "there is" for everything. Japanese splits it into two verbs depending on whether the thing can move on its own: ある for objects, plants, and events, いる for people and animals.

### ある: Things That Don't Move Themselves

<JSentenceBlock furigana="この町[まち]に公園[こうえん]がありますか。" romaji="Kono machi ni kouen ga arimasu ka." english="Is there a park in this town?" />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="水曜日[すいようび]に日本語[にほんご]のクラスで漢字[かんじ]テストがあります。" romaji="Suiyoubi ni nihongo no kurasu de kanji tesuto ga arimasu." english="There's a kanji test in Japanese class on Wednesday." />

<Hint>ある covers more than physical objects, it also covers scheduled things like tests, parties, and meetings. A test doesn't move on its own, so it gets ある the same way a park or a book does.</Hint>

### いる: Things That Move Themselves

<JSentenceBlock furigana="あそこに田中[たなか]さんがいますよ。" romaji="Asoko ni Tanaka-san ga imasu yo." english="Tanaka is over there." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="ケニアにはライオンがたくさんいます。" romaji="Kenia ni wa raion ga takusan imasu." english="Kenya has a lot of lions." />

<Hint>A tree standing in a park is あります, the same tree with a bird sitting in it makes the bird います, since the bird can fly off on its own and the tree can't. Robots and cars, despite moving, still take ある, the test is whether something has its own will, not whether it's technically capable of motion.</Hint>

### Having, Not Just Existing

<JSentenceBlock furigana="姉[あね]は女[おんな]の子[こ]が二人[ふたり]います。" romaji="Ane wa onna no ko ga futari imasu." english="My older sister has two daughters." />

<Hint>ある and いる both double as "to have," not just "there is." 姉は女の子が二人います literally reads "as for older sister, two daughters exist," which is how Japanese says someone has children. The daughters are people, so they still take いる even though the sentence is really about possession, not location.</Hint>
