Many Japanese sentences carry two separate markers: は sets the overall topic, someone or something the sentence is about, while が marks the specific part or quality actually being described.

<JSentenceBlock furigana="由紀[ゆき]さんは目[め]が大[おお]きいです。" romaji="Yuki-san wa me ga ookii desu." english="Yuki has big eyes." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="直樹[なおき]さんは髪[かみ]が短[みじか]いです。" romaji="Naoki-san wa kami ga mijikai desu." english="Naoki has short hair." />

<Hint>由紀さんは目が大きい doesn't put 大きい directly on 由紀さん, it's specifically her eyes that are big, not her overall size. は names the whole person as the topic; が narrows in on the one feature the sentence is actually commenting on.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="拓也[たくや]さんはスポーツが得意[とくい]です。" romaji="Takuya-san wa supootsu ga tokui desu." english="Takuya is good at sports." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="沙耶[さや]さんは料理[りょうり]が苦手[にがて]です。" romaji="Saya-san wa ryouri ga nigate desu." english="Saya isn't good at cooking." />

<Hint>得意 and 苦手, like 好き and 上手 before them, always take が on the specific skill or activity, even though は sets the person as the overall topic. This same double-marker shape is what makes ほしい (Xがほしい) and わかる (Xがわかる) work the way you learned them, が landing on whatever's specifically wanted or understood, は on the person feeling or knowing it.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="スーパーはどこが安[やす]いですか。" romaji="Suupaa wa doko ga yasui desu ka." english="Which supermarket is cheap?" />

<Hint>Even a question word like どこ can take が in this slot, どこが安いですか asking which one, out of the supermarkets already understood as the topic, has the specific quality of being cheap.</Hint>
