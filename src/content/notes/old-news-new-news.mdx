By now 은/는 and 이/가 have both shown up constantly without much explanation of why one appeared instead of the other. Here's the actual rule. 이/가 marks the subject when it's new or unknown information, the thing you're pointing out for the first time. 은/는 marks the topic when it's already known, the thing the conversation has settled on talking about. English doesn't force this choice. Korean makes you make it in every single sentence.

### Introducing vs. Commenting

<SentenceBlock formal="여기 사과가 있습니다." formalRom="yeogi sagaga isseumnida." polite="여기 사과가 있어요." politeRom="yeogi sagaga isseoyo." casual="여기 사과가 있어." casualRom="yeogi sagaga isseo." english="Here's an apple." />

<SentenceBlock formal="그 사과는 맛있습니다." formalRom="geu saganeun masisseumnida." polite="그 사과는 맛있어요." politeRom="geu saganeun masisseoyo." casual="그 사과는 맛있어." casualRom="geu saganeun masisseo." english="That apple is delicious." />

<Hint>This is the classic story-opening pattern in Korean: 가 to introduce a character or object for the first time, 는 for every mention after that. The first line of a fairy tale almost always uses 가; every sentence about that same character afterward switches to 는.</Hint>

### Answering "Who"

Question words like 누구 (who) almost always pull 이/가, because the answer is by definition new information. 누구 even contracts with 가 irregularly, dropping a syllable: 누구가 becomes 누가.

<SentenceBlock formal="누가 선생님입니까?" formalRom="nuga seonsaengnimimnikka?" polite="누가 선생님이에요?" politeRom="nuga seonsaengnimieyo?" casual="누가 선생님이야?" casualRom="nuga seonsaengnimiya?" english="Who is the teacher?" />

<SentenceBlock formal="안나 씨가 선생님입니다." formalRom="anna ssiga seonsaengnimimnida." polite="안나 씨가 선생님이에요." politeRom="anna ssiga seonsaengnimieyo." casual="안나 씨가 선생님이야." casualRom="anna ssiga seonsaengnimiya." english="Anna is the teacher." />

Swap that same sentence to 는 and the meaning shifts. 안나 씨는 선생님이에요 doesn't answer "who's the teacher," it responds to something like "what does Anna do?" Anna is already the topic; her job is the new part.

<SentenceBlock formal="안나 씨는 선생님입니다." formalRom="anna ssineun seonsaengnimimnida." polite="안나 씨는 선생님이에요." politeRom="anna ssineun seonsaengnimieyo." casual="안나 씨는 선생님이야." casualRom="anna ssineun seonsaengnimiya." english="As for Anna, she's the teacher." />

### Contrast

는 has a second job that has nothing to do with old versus new information: setting up a contrast between two things.

<SentenceBlock formal="저는 커피를 좋아합니다." formalRom="jeoneun keopireul joahamnida." polite="저는 커피를 좋아해요." politeRom="jeoneun keopireul joahaeyo." casual="나는 커피를 좋아해." casualRom="naneun keopireul joahae." english="I like coffee." />

<SentenceBlock formal="동생은 차를 좋아합니다." formalRom="dongsaengeun chareul joahamnida." polite="동생은 차를 좋아해요." politeRom="dongsaengeun chareul joahaeyo." casual="동생은 차를 좋아해." casualRom="dongsaengeun chareul joahae." english="My younger sibling likes tea." />

<Hint>Said back to back like this, 는 quietly implies "unlike me." Two 는 markers in back-to-back sentences almost always signal a comparison, even without a word for "but" anywhere in the sentence. This is why 는 can sound pointed in a way 이/가 never does: naming someone as a topic right after naming someone else invites the listener to compare them.</Hint>

### One Common Mix-Up

좋아하다 (to like, an action) and 좋다 (to be good, a description) look related and get confused constantly, but they don't take the same particle. 좋아하다 acts like an ordinary verb and takes 을/를 on what you like, exactly like 커피를 좋아해요 above. 좋다 describes a quality instead of naming an action, so whatever's pleasing takes 이/가, the same subject-marking job this whole lesson has been about.

<SentenceBlock formal="저는 커피가 좋습니다." formalRom="jeoneun keopiga josseumnida." polite="저는 커피가 좋아요." politeRom="jeoneun keopiga joayo." casual="나는 커피가 좋아." casualRom="naneun keopiga joa." english="I like coffee. (literally: coffee is pleasing to me)" />

<Hint type="mistake">저는 커피를 좋아해요 and 저는 커피가 좋아요 both mean "I like coffee," but the particles aren't interchangeable between them: 커피를 좋아요 and 커피가 좋아해요 are both wrong. 를 belongs with 좋아하다 (좋아해요, 좋아하고, 좋아하지만...); 가 belongs with 좋다 (좋아요, 좋고, 좋지만...). This is one of the most common particle mistakes beginners make, precisely because 좋아해요 and 좋아요 sound so close to each other.</Hint>
