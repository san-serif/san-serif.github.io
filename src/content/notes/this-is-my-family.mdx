Korean marks more than just the subject of a sentence. Attach 은 or 는 to a person or thing and you're announcing the topic, the thing the rest of the sentence is about to comment on: **subject + 은/는 + job + 이에요/예요** is the whole formula for saying who someone is. It also splits "I" into two words depending on the job that word is doing: 저 when it's out front acting, 제 when it's tucked in front of a noun showing possession.

### 저는 vs 제

<SentenceBlock formal="저는 엘리입니다." formalRom="jeoneun elliimnida." polite="저는 엘리예요." politeRom="jeoneun elliyeyo." casual="나는 엘리야." casualRom="naneun elliya." english="I'm Ellie." />

<SentenceBlock formal="제 이름은 엘리입니다." formalRom="je ireumeun elliimnida." polite="제 이름은 엘리예요." politeRom="je ireumeun elliyeyo." casual="내 이름은 엘리야." casualRom="nae ireumeun elliya." english="My name is Ellie." />

Notice the casual row changed more than the ending. 저 and 제 are the polite, lowered forms of "I" and "my," the ones you use with strangers and teachers. Talking to a friend, they drop to 나 and 내.

### This Is My Friend

이 사람 (this person) works like a placeholder you can swap for anyone. Once you've pointed at someone, 은/는 hands the topic over to their name, their job, or whoever they belong to.

<SentenceBlock formal="이 사람은 가수입니다." formalRom="i sarameun gasuimnida." polite="이 사람은 가수예요." politeRom="i sarameun gasuyeyo." casual="이 사람은 가수야." casualRom="i sarameun gasuya." english="This person is a singer." />

<SentenceBlock formal="제 친구는 요리사입니다." formalRom="je chinguneun yorisaimnida." polite="제 친구는 요리사예요." politeRom="je chinguneun yorisayeyo." casual="내 친구는 요리사야." casualRom="nae chinguneun yorisaya." english="My friend is a chef." />

<SentenceBlock formal="제 동생은 학생입니다." formalRom="je dongsaengeun haksaengimnida." polite="제 동생은 학생이에요." politeRom="je dongsaengeun haksaengieyo." casual="내 동생은 학생이야." casualRom="nae dongsaengeun haksaengiya." english="My younger sibling is a student." />

<Hint>씨 (attached to a full name or given name, like 안나 씨 or 지혜 씨) is the default polite title for another adult, roughly as neutral as "Mr./Ms." But it only pairs with a given name. Attaching it to a surname alone, like 김 씨, sounds blunt or even a little condescending, the opposite of how "Mr. Kim" reads in English.</Hint>

### Yes, My Mother Is a Doctor

Family words swap out just as easily, though a few change entirely once you're speaking casually. 어머니 turns into 엄마 the moment the conversation relaxes, the same shift English makes from "mother" to "mom."

<SentenceBlock formal="어머니는 의사입니다." formalRom="eomeonineun uisaimnida." polite="어머니는 의사예요." politeRom="eomeonineun uisayeyo." casual="엄마는 의사야." casualRom="eommaneun uisaya." english="My mother is a doctor." />

<SentenceBlock formal="형은 가수입니다." formalRom="hyeongeun gasuimnida." polite="형은 가수예요." politeRom="hyeongeun gasuyeyo." casual="형은 가수야." casualRom="hyeongeun gasuya." english="My older brother is a singer." />

<SentenceBlock formal="이 사람은 경찰입니다." formalRom="i sarameun gyeongcharimnida." polite="이 사람은 경찰이에요." politeRom="i sarameun gyeongcharieyo." casual="이 사람은 경찰이야." casualRom="i sarameun gyeongchariya." english="This person is a police officer." />

<SentenceBlock formal="안나 씨의 직업은 학생입니다." formalRom="anna ssiui jigeobeun haksaengimnida." polite="안나 씨의 직업은 학생이에요." politeRom="anna ssiui jigeobeun haksaengieyo." casual="안나 씨 직업은 학생이야." casualRom="anna ssi jigeobeun haksaengiya." english="Anna's job is being a student." />

<SentenceBlock formal="서울은 한국의 수도입니다." formalRom="seoureun hangugui sudoimnida." polite="서울은 한국의 수도예요." politeRom="seoureun hangugui sudoyeyo." casual="서울은 한국 수도야." casualRom="seoureun hanguk sudoya." english="Seoul is the capital of Korea." />

<Hint>Koreans essentially never call an older sibling by their first name, even as adults with kids of their own. A younger brother calls his older sister 누나 for life, not her name. It's less like a nickname and more like the relationship itself replaces the name in conversation.</Hint>
