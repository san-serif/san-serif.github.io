がる attaches to a feeling adjective's stem and turns it into a verb describing someone else's visible behavior, shivering, complaining, reaching for something. It's the mechanism behind 欲しがる and たがる, which you've already met, now with the full pattern spelled out.

<JSentenceBlock furigana="弟[おとうと]はいつも寒[さむ]がっています。" romaji="Otouto wa itsumo samugatteimasu." english="My younger brother always seems to be cold." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="弟[おとうと]は毎日[まいにち]サッカーをしたがっています。" romaji="Otouto wa mainichi sakkaa o shitagatteimasu." english="My younger brother always wants to play soccer." />

<Hint>寒い (cold) describes your own felt sensation directly. 寒がる describes someone else acting cold, shivering, pulling on a jacket, the visible signs rather than a direct claim about their inner state. This is the same restriction Korean's 있다/없다 emotion words run into and Japanese solves the same way: you can only state your own feelings plainly, everyone else's have to be reported from the outside.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="弟[おとうと]はいつも寂[さび]しがっているから、可愛[かわい]い猫[ねこ]をあげようと思[おも]っています。" romaji="Otouto wa itsumo sabishigatteiru kara, kawaii neko o ageyou to omotteimasu." english="My younger brother always seems lonely, so I'm thinking of giving him a cute cat." />

<Hint>がる conjugates like an ordinary verb once attached, not like the adjective it came from: 寒がる, 寒がらない, 寒がった, 寒がらなかった. 面白い (interesting) becomes 面白がる (to act like something's interesting, to find something amusing), following the exact same shift.</Hint>
