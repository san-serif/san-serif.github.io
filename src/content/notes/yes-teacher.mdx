Every language has a polite way to give an instruction and a blunt way to give an order. Korean keeps both in the same verb. Swap the ending on -으세요 and the sentence stays word-for-word identical in meaning, it just changes who's allowed to say it to whom.

### The Classroom Suffix

Verb + **세요** is the core rule: attach it to a verb stem and you've turned it into "please [verb]." -(으)세요 is the sound of every Korean classroom, a command soft enough to still be a request. Push it up to -(으)십시오 and you get the version printed on airport signage and heard from flight attendants. Strip it down to the bare verb stem and you get what you'd actually say to a close friend, no cushioning at all.

<Hint>Dropping all the way to the bare casual form can land as a real order, not a friendly request, if your tone is flat. Koreans soften casual commands with 좀 ("a little," functioning like "please" here) instead: 이거 좀 줘 ("give me this, would you") feels like an actual request between friends, where 이거 줘 alone can sound like you're barking at someone.</Hint>

### Please Give Me

There's a second command pattern built on 주다 (to give): attach **주세요** to a noun, or to a verb's connective -아/어 form, to mean "please give me [noun]" or "please [verb] for me." It's the single most useful phrase in the language for anything from ordering food to asking a classmate for help.

<SentenceBlock formal="물 주십시오." formalRom="mul jusipsio." polite="물 주세요." politeRom="mul juseyo." casual="물 줘." casualRom="mul jwo." english="Please give me water." />

<SentenceBlock formal="펜 주십시오." formalRom="pen jusipsio." polite="펜 주세요." politeRom="pen juseyo." casual="펜 줘." casualRom="pen jwo." english="Please give me a pen." />

### Thank You, I'm Sorry

고맙다 and 미안하다 run through all three registers cleanly, the same way 이다 does. But you'll also hear 감사합니다 and 죄송합니다 constantly, and they're not casual-to-formal steps of anything. They're separate, more deferential words that were borrowed straight from Chinese roots (감사 = 感謝, "gratitude"; 죄송 = 罪悚, "guilt and fear"), and Korean never really relaxes them into casual speech. You say them in -습니다 or not at all.

<SentenceBlock formal="고맙습니다." formalRom="gomapseumnida." polite="고마워요." politeRom="gomawoyo." casual="고마워." casualRom="gomawo." english="Thank you." />

<SentenceBlock formal="미안합니다." formalRom="mianhamnida." polite="미안해요." politeRom="mianhaeyo." casual="미안해." casualRom="mianhae." english="I'm sorry." />

<Hint>This is the one place formal register actually shows up constantly in daily life. Even close friends will throw a quick 고맙습니다 or 죄송합니다 at a stranger, a shopkeeper, or a stranger's dog they just bumped into. Politeness toward people you don't know overrides whatever register you'd normally use with your friends.</Hint>
