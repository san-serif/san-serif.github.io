In one conversation, your friend can point out whose book it is, ask if you’ve eaten, suggest sitting down, and ask what you think. Chinese uses short particles to do those jobs: 的 links or marks possession, 地 connects a manner to an action, 了 marks completion or change, and 吗、吧、呢 shape questions and suggestions. The examples show each particle in its own sentence.

### Examples

<CSentenceBlock simplified="这是我的书。" traditional="這是我的書。" pinyin="Zhè shì wǒ de shū." jyutping="ze5 si6 ngo5 dik1 syu1." english="This is my book." />

<CSentenceBlock simplified="她慢慢地说。" traditional="她慢慢地說。" pinyin="Tā mànmàn de shuō." jyutping="taa1 maan6 maan6 dei6 syut3." english="She speaks slowly." />

<CSentenceBlock simplified="我们吃了饭。" traditional="我們吃了飯。" pinyin="Wǒmen chī le fàn." jyutping="ngo5 mun4 sik6 zo2 faan6." english="We’ve eaten." />

<CSentenceBlock simplified="你呢？我们走吧。" traditional="你呢？我們走吧。" pinyin="Nǐ ne? Wǒmen zǒu ba." jyutping="nei5 ne1? ngo5 mun4 zau2 baa6." english="What about you? Let’s go." />

<Hint type="hint">The two 了 entries are related but not identical: one marks an event as completed, while sentence-final 了 often marks a change or new situation. Learn them from full sentences.</Hint>
