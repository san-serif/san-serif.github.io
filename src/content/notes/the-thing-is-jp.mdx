んです attaches after the plain form and frames a statement as background, an explanation, or the reason behind something the listener can already sense, rather than a plain, standalone fact. な-adjectives and nouns take なんです instead of plain んです.

<JSentenceBlock furigana="実[じつ]は彼女[かのじょ]と初[はじ]めてのデートなんです。" romaji="Jitsu wa kanojo to hajimete no deeto nan desu." english="Actually, it's my first date with my girlfriend." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="仕事[しごと]が決[き]まって嬉[うれ]しいんです。" romaji="Shigoto ga kimatte ureshii n desu." english="I got a job, and I'm happy about it." />

<Hint>Compare 嬉しいです, a plain statement of fact, with 嬉しいんです, which implies there's a story or a reason behind the feeling worth knowing. んです invites the listener into the background, why you're happy, why you're declining, why something is the way it is.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="どうして食[た]べないんですか。" romaji="Doushite tabenai n desu ka." english="How come you're not eating?" />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="ワンさんが来[き]ませんね。どうしたんですか。" romaji="Wan-san ga kimasen ne. Dou shita n desu ka." english="Wang isn't here. What happened?" />

<Hint>んですか asks for the story behind an observed situation, not a plain fact, どうしたんですか asks specifically because something visibly seems off, more natural here than a plain どうしましたか, which would sound oddly detached from what you're noticing.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="あのう、パソコンが壊[こわ]れたんですが。。。" romaji="Anou, pasokon ga kowareta n desu ga..." english="Um, my computer broke, so..." />

<Hint>んですが, trailing off unfinished, is an extremely common way to open a request or complaint, laying out the background first and letting the listener infer what you need, softer and more natural than stating the request outright.</Hint>
