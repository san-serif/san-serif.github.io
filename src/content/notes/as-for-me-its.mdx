は sets a topic and だ/です closes the sentence, but the noun in between doesn't have to literally equal the topic. Japanese is comfortable leaving the real relationship, location, time, price, whatever, unstated and letting context supply it.

<JSentenceBlock furigana="出身[しゅっしん]はカナダです。" romaji="Shusshin wa Kanada desu." english="I'm from Canada." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="趣味[しゅみ]はテニスです。" romaji="Shumi wa tenisu desu." english="My hobby is tennis." />

<Hint>These read naturally as X is Y, straightforward identification, 出身 (hometown) is Canada, 趣味 (hobby) is tennis, no surprises yet.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="試合[しあい]は土曜日[どようび]です。" romaji="Shiai wa doyoubi desu." english="The match is on Saturday." />

<JSentenceBlock furigana="試合[しあい]は湯[ゆ]あ大学[だいがく]のサッカー場[じょう]です。" romaji="Shiai wa Yua daigaku no sakkaa jou desu." english="The match is at Yua University's soccer field." />

<Hint>試合は土曜日です doesn't literally mean "the match equals Saturday," it means "the match happens on Saturday," the relationship (when, where) left for context to fill in. This is why the pattern is sometimes nicknamed the "unagi sentence," after the classic example 僕はうなぎだ, "I'll have the eel" (literally "I am eel"), said by someone ordering food, not claiming to be an eel.</Hint>

<JSentenceBlock furigana="私[わたし]は図書館[としょかん]です。" romaji="Watashi wa toshokan desu." english="Mine is the library. / I'll be at the library." />

<Hint>Answering どこで会いましょうか, "where should we meet," with 私は図書館です doesn't claim "I am a library," it fills in whatever relationship the question set up, here, "where I'll be." The pattern leans hard on shared context, and gets used constantly once you're used to spotting it.</Hint>
