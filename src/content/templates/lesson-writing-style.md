# San Serif lesson writing style

These conventions apply to Japanese, Korean, and Chinese grammar lessons. Follow the shared lesson components and language-specific formatting already used by the site.

This is the shared editorial guide. Keep language-specific files for script, readings, examples, and curriculum details only. Korean lessons also follow [the Korean lesson specification](./korean-lesson-style-notes.md).

## The reader's experience

- Teach one useful decision at a time. Let the learner picture when they might need the form before explaining its label.
- Write to the learner as a capable person trying to say something. Prefer a concrete situation, a meaningful contrast, or a small conversational problem to a dictionary-style definition.
- Give each lesson a natural shape for its topic. A dialogue, contrast, short sequence, common mistake, or real-life usage note can lead when it serves the grammar. Do not force every feature into every lesson.
- Make examples earn their place. They should sound like things someone could actually say, and the set should reveal how the form works across a few useful situations.
- Explain the target clearly before adding detail. Use a memorable analogy when it makes a distinction easier to hold onto; keep the explanation accurate after the analogy.
- Keep the voice warm, direct, and lightly playful. Use humor only when it comes naturally from the example. Avoid jokes that distract from the language.
- Keep prose specific and economical. Remove generic prompts, curriculum metadata, repeated definitions, empty transitions, and instructions that tell the learner to notice something without explaining what to notice.

## Lesson titles and sections

- Use an English title in title case, with a short native-language form in parentheses when useful. Do not list every form covered in a lesson.
- Keep section headings in English. Name the teaching job of the section; add a short form only when it helps orient the reader.
- Vary section order and heading choice to fit the lesson. Retain the shared vocabulary section and page controls.
- Write descriptions for learners: one useful example or contrast, then the lesson's payoff. Keep them concise and avoid source-record or HSK bookkeeping.

## Explanations, examples, and callouts

- Start with a scene, question, contrast, or clear takeaway. Avoid formulaic openings such as “This lesson covers…” and “The pattern is used to…”.
- Move from the learner's decision to the form, then show it in examples. Build a mini-dialogue or a sequence when the grammar is conversational or temporal; use contrast when two forms are easy to confuse.
- Keep explanations beside the examples they explain. Avoid long blocks of terminology before the learner has seen the form in action.
- Use **Hints** for a practical rule, common mistake, limit, or contrast. Use **In Real Life** for a grounded note about natural phrasing, register, or context. A callout should add a new, specific insight.
- Cross-reference a related lesson only when the comparison helps. Name the relationship in the sentence and link to the actual lesson.
- Keep cultural claims narrow, sourced where needed, and free of claims about what all speakers do.

## Vocabulary and language-specific details

- Choose vocabulary that supports the examples and the situation. Every Korean lesson must include **at least 20 distinct, useful vocabulary entries**, with Korean, romanization, and a concise English gloss. Do not add unrelated words to fill the count. Audit every Korean lesson before delivery, including existing lessons revised during the task.
- Preserve the required vocabulary count and deep links for Chinese. Check every script, reading, and gloss against the intended meaning.
- Keep Japanese and Korean readings and forms consistent with their existing components. Do not add romanization or script controls to article prose.
- For Chinese, provide both Simplified and Traditional characters and clean accented Pinyin/Jyutping readings through the existing controls. Keep toggles self-explanatory.
- Keep source IDs, levels, and curriculum mapping in metadata. They are editorial aids, not lesson content.

## Editorial review

Before a lesson is considered revised, read the full page in order. Check that its title promises what it teaches, every example supports the target, explanations progress rather than repeat, callouts add something useful, and vocabulary belongs to the lesson. Preserve valid grammar and existing links while improving voice and flow.
